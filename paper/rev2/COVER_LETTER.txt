Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript entitled "700-km intercity
Yb/Sr optical clock comparison at 2.3×10⁻¹⁸" for
consideration as an Article in Nature.

Optical clocks now reach fractional systematic uncertainties at or
below 2×10⁻¹⁸, yet the redefinition of the SI second requires these
self-assessed budgets to be validated by independent frequency-ratio
comparisons between clocks of different types in different
laboratories, at an overall uncertainty below 5×10⁻¹⁸. So far, such
agreement has been demonstrated only between clocks on a single
campus. We report the first intercity, cross-species measurement to
meet this requirement: an ytterbium lattice clock at IAPMST in Wuhan
and a strontium lattice clock at USTC in Shanghai, about 700 km apart,
compared over a phase-stabilized 1 350 km fiber link carried on a
non-exclusive commercial telecom network. We determine the Yb/Sr
frequency ratio to be 1.207 507 039 343 337 720 1(28), with a total
standard uncertainty of 2.34×10⁻¹⁸, consistent with the NIST–JILA
campus value within the combined standard uncertainty. Together with
the NIST–JILA result, this provides the second, independent
determination of the Yb/Sr ratio by a different institute, as required
by criterion I.2.b of the redefinition roadmap for this pair of
transitions.

Three features of the work go beyond the frequency ratio itself and
address questions of broad significance. First, the measurement shows
that the transfer infrastructure is no longer the bottleneck at this
level: the fiber link and the two frequency combs contribute well
below the clocks, and the remaining budget is dominated by the
per-segment statistics and by the geodetic determination of the
gravitational potential difference between the sites. Second, we
document the time-varying gravitational redshift with two independent
tide models, which degrade the fit when applied as corrections and
disagree in sign; we therefore carry the term as a data-driven
uncertainty item rather than a correction, and discuss the two-way
relation between clock comparisons and tide-model validation. Third,
the comparison demonstrates the operational character that a future
optical-clock network will need: over a two-month campaign the clocks
were restarted repeatedly, yet the segment-level estimates show no
systematic offset between measuring stages, establishing the two sites
as a working node pair and a milestone on the necessary path toward a
wide-area optical-clock network. All numbers in the manuscript are
generated by a reproducible pipeline from a frozen, audited result
layer; the analysis code and derived data are available at
https://github.com/hjiang555-a11y/gravitation-whsh under the Apache
License 2.0.

We believe this work will be of broad interest to Nature's readership:
it addresses a central requirement of the redefinition of the SI
second, and it demonstrates, at intercity scale, the class of
measurement on which that redefinition, and the applications that
follow it, depend.
