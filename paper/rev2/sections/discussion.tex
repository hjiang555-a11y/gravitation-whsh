The value reported here agrees with the two existing measurements of
the Yb/Sr ratio at the level set by the redefinition roadmap
(Figure~\ref{fig:history}): relative to the NIST--JILA value
\refNistV(\refNistU)~\citep{aeppli2026nist} our central value differs
by $\dVsNistE$, and relative to the European-network value
\refEuV(\refEuU)~\citep{pizzocaro2026european} by $\dVsEuE$, both well
within the \refThreshold{} agreement threshold of the roadmap's
frequency-ratio
criterion~\citep{dimarcq2024roadmap, cctf2025taskforce}. To our
knowledge, this is the first intercity, cross-species frequency-ratio
measurement meeting that requirement (as of \claimDate{}).

The three independent Yb/Sr values also track the progress of the
comparison technology itself: from the first measurement in
2014~\citep{akamatsu2014ybsr} and its refinement to
\nemitzU~\citep{nemitz2016ybsr}, through the BACON campus
network~\citep{beloy2021bacon} and the European fiber
network~\citep{pizzocaro2026european}, to \refSameCampusU~on a single
campus~\citep{aeppli2026nist}. Our measurement closes this sequence at
intercity scale with $\uTotalE\times10^{-18}$, and the three
latest, independently obtained values agree at the few-$10^{-18}$
level. The convergence works in both directions: each comparison
validates the others, and the agreement feeds back into the
evaluation of the methods and error budgets of the individual
laboratories---what the redefinition needs is no longer a single
laboratory's claim about its own clock, but the agreement between
independent measurements.

The final uncertainty is comparable to, and slightly below, that of
the NIST--JILA campus comparison, even though the present measurement
operates on a distributed system that carries additional uncertainty
from the fiber link, the two frequency combs and the geodetic
potential difference. The compensating factor is time: the
$\nHours$-hour campaign record is considerably longer than a typical
single-campus measurement window, and longer continuous averaging is
the direct route to a smaller statistical term, provided the record
remains jump-free. Keeping the record clean was itself a technical
achievement of the campaign, made possible by the jump-suppression and
monitoring controls on the fiber link; the resulting segments carry
almost no rejected samples.

Changes in the Earth's gravitational potential enter the comparison
directly. The static difference between the two clock transitions is
fixed by first-order spirit leveling and applied as a correction; of
the time-varying contributions, the tidal term is already appreciable
at our precision and is therefore carried as a data-driven
uncertainty item rather than corrected
(Section~\ref{sec:results-budget}), while co-seismic steps from the
regional seismicity during the campaign fall well below the reported
precision. The effect grows with the scale of the comparison: on
longer, continental- and intercontinental-scale baselines the
time-varying redshift becomes one of the dominant systematic
terms~\citep{denker2018geodetic}. That same sensitivity turns the
network into an instrument for the Earth: clock comparisons over
large baselines directly sense the time-variable gravitational
potential, providing an independent means of testing and calibrating
tide and loading models at the $10^{-18}$ level---the
chronometric-geodesy applications proposed for optical-clock
networks~\citep{grotti2018geodesy, mcgrew2018geodesy,
takamoto2020redshift}. Conversely, tidal knowledge gained in this way
feeds back into the comparisons themselves: with data-consistent
models, the time-varying term can be corrected rather than carried,
extending the accuracy reach of large-scale clock comparisons. A
fully self-consistent treatment of the time-dependent potential
would require a dedicated geodetic campaign with in-situ gravimetry
and site-specific loading models and is left to future work.

The campaign also demonstrates the operational character that a
future optical-clock network will need. The reproducibility of the
reported ratio rests on more than the retained record: before the
comparison, the two clocks were deliberately switched off and
restarted to test their restart reproducibility, and the laboratory
environments were kept under tight control, so that the clocks'
ability to return to the same frequency is itself a tested property
of the measurement. Over the two-month campaign the two clocks were
then restarted, the transfer chain re-locked, and the beat record
interrupted many times; the segment-level ratio estimates show no
evidence of a systematic offset between the three measuring stages,
so the reproducibility of the comparison does not depend on
continuous operation of either system. This is the practical
counterpart of the coordinated multi-clock
campaigns~\citep{lindvall2025coordinated, pizzocaro2026european}: the
network tolerates interruption, provided each retained window is
self-consistently characterized.

Several limitations bound the present result. First, the per-segment
statistical uncertainties are the dominant budget term, and five of
the sixteen segments carry model-limited slope diagnostics; the
random-effect treatment accounts for any remaining excess scatter at
the level of the reported uncertainty, but a longer record would
reduce the statistical term. Second, the systematic evaluations of
the two clocks are those published by the laboratories; independent
verification of those budgets, as opposed to the transfer, is not
attempted here. Third, the geopotential difference rests on a single
first-order leveling campaign: the available GNSS-plus-geoid-model
calibration is too imprecise (Methods) to act as an effective
cross-check, and closing this gap will require transportable optical
clocks with higher frequency reproducibility, rather than geodetic
techniques alone; the provenance of the supplied tide series and the
exact accounting of the full sample totals remain external items
pending confirmation. Finally, the present campaign meets the
uncertainty requirement of the roadmap for the Yb/Sr ratio, and with
the NIST--JILA measurement it provides the second independent
determination of this ratio by a different institute; the full
criterion I.2.b additionally requires at least five non-unit ratios,
each measured at least twice in this way.

Looking ahead, the result establishes this link as a prototype node
pair of a distributed optical-clock network: the same infrastructure
can be extended to further nodes, and the technique demonstrated here
(clocks at different sites agreeing at the redefinition threshold) is
the operation that both the redefinition and post-redefinition
applications require. The building blocks for such a network are now
in place: terrestrial fiber links of the kind used
here~\citep{schioppo2022cavities, chen2026dissemination} carry
comparisons over thousands of kilometers within continents, while
free-space and satellite-based links are closing the remaining
gaps~\citep{pizzocaro2021vlbi, shen2021satellite,
shen2022freespace, caldwell2023geo}. The natural next step is a hybrid architecture in
which optical-clock nodes on continental fiber backbones are
interconnected across oceans by satellite or free-space optical links,
so that any pair of clocks on Earth can be compared at the level
demonstrated here. Realizing it will put the same demands on geodetic
infrastructure that this comparison
faced~\citep{grotti2018geodesy, mcgrew2018geodesy}.
